## Key points

- About **5,900 Riverton households (14%)** cannot buy wireline internet service at the federal benchmark speed of 100/20 Mbps, and roughly one in five low-income households has no home internet subscription at all.
- Price, not only availability, is the main barrier: the median advertised price for 100 Mbps service in Riverton is **$78 per month**, and the federal Affordable Connectivity Program that offset $30 of that for 6,100 local households ended in 2024.
- Cities have four broad options, ranging from a city-owned retail fiber utility to targeted subsidies with no new infrastructure. They differ sharply in cost, risk, and control.
- **We recommend a phased, open-access approach:** build a city-owned fiber backbone, pilot open-access fiber in the two least-served neighborhoods with federal BEAD and state matching funds, and fund a digital equity program now.

## 1. The problem

Reliable, affordable home internet is now a precondition for remote work, school, telehealth, and access to public services. The Federal Communications Commission raised its benchmark for fixed broadband from 25/3 Mbps to **100 Mbps download and 20 Mbps upload** in March 2024.[^fcc2024]

Measured against that benchmark, Riverton has gaps in both coverage and adoption:

| Indicator | Riverton | State | Source |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Households without any wireline option at 100/20 Mbps | 14% | 9% | FCC National Broadband Map, June 2026 data[^fccmap] |
| Households with only one provider at 100/20 Mbps | 41% | 33% | FCC National Broadband Map |
| Households with no home internet subscription | 11% | 8% | American Community Survey, 2024 5-year[^acs] |
| ... among households earning under $35,000 | 21% | 17% | American Community Survey, 2024 5-year |
| Median advertised monthly price, 100 Mbps tier | $78 | $71 | City price survey, August 2026 |

The unserved addresses are concentrated in two areas: the **Eastbank** neighborhood, where older aerial cable plant was never upgraded, and the semi-rural **North Ridge** annexation area. The adoption gap is concentrated in **Eastbank** and **Mill District**, which have the city's lowest median incomes.

Where there is only one provider, there is little pressure on price. Comparing provider price lists, households in single-provider areas of Riverton pay on average $14 more per month for comparable speeds than households with a choice of two or more.

## 2. The changing federal and state context

**Funding is available, but time-limited.** The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act created the $42.45 billion Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment (BEAD) program, administered by the states.[^bead] Our state's final proposal allows local governments and public–private partnerships to apply for subgrants for unserved and underserved locations, with a minimum 25% local match. The second subgrant round opens in March 2027.

**The affordability subsidy is gone.** The Affordable Connectivity Program provided up to $30 per month to about 23 million households nationally before its funding ran out; it stopped accepting enrollments in February 2024 and ended on June 1, 2024.[^acp] About 6,100 Riverton households were enrolled. A city survey of former recipients found that 18% had since dropped their home service and another 31% had downgraded to a cheaper, slower plan.

**State law permits a municipal role.** Roughly 16 states restrict or prohibit municipal broadband networks.[^preemption] Ours is not among them: the 2021 amendments to the Municipal Utilities Act allow cities to own and operate telecommunications facilities, subject to a public hearing and a requirement that a retail service be self-supporting within ten years.

## 3. What other cities have done

Municipal broadband has a mixed record, and the outcomes depend more on governance and financing than on technology.

**Chattanooga, Tennessee.** The city's electric utility, EPB, built a citywide fiber network beginning in 2010, partly to support a smart electric grid. An independent study estimated that the network generated about $2.69 billion in economic and social benefits in its first decade, including roughly 9,500 jobs and reduced outage costs, against a cost of about $396 million.[^lobo2020]

**Longmont, Colorado.** Voters approved $45.3 million in revenue bonds in 2013 to build NextLight, a city-owned fiber service offered through the electric utility. The network reached its subscriber targets ahead of schedule and has consistently ranked among the fastest and best-rated providers in national consumer surveys.[^longmont]

**Provo, Utah, and Burlington, Vermont.** Not every effort succeeded. Provo built iProvo for about $39 million and eventually sold it to Google Fiber in 2013 for $1, with residents continuing to repay the original bonds.[^provo] Burlington Telecom accumulated tens of millions of dollars in debt, used city funds without authorization, and was sued by its lender before the system was sold in 2018.[^burlington] Both cases involved optimistic take-rate assumptions and weak separation between the network's finances and the city's.

**Open-access models.** In Ammon, Idaho, and in the UTOPIA Fiber network serving several Utah cities, the public entity owns the fiber and private internet providers compete to sell service over it.[^openaccess] This keeps the city out of retail operations while creating competition on a single physical network.

## 4. Policy options

| | **A. City retail fiber utility** | **B. Open-access network** | **C. Public–private partnership** | **D. Digital equity program only** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **What the city does** | Builds and operates fiber; sells service to residents | Builds and owns fiber; leases access to private ISPs | Provides conduit, rights-of-way, or co-investment; partner builds and operates | Subsidizes service, devices, and digital skills; no new infrastructure |
| **Capital cost to city** | High (about $68M citywide) | High (similar to A), lower if phased | Low to moderate | Low ($1.2M to $1.8M per year) |
| **Financial risk to city** | High: depends on take rate | Moderate: depends on ISP lease revenue | Low to moderate | Low |
| **Time to first service** | 18 to 24 months | 18 to 24 months | 12 to 36 months; depends on partner | Immediate |
| **Control over price and equity** | Full | Strong (through wholesale terms) | Limited to contract terms | Limited |
| **Addresses coverage gap?** | Yes | Yes | Partly | No |
| **Addresses affordability gap?** | Yes | Yes, through competition | Partly | Yes, while funded |

: Comparison of policy options for Riverton.

### Financial outline for a citywide build

Engineering consultants estimated the cost of a citywide fiber-to-the-home network in March 2026:

| Component | Basis | Estimate |
|---|---|---:|
| Backbone and distribution | 42,000 passings × $1,250 per passing | $52.5 million |
| Customer connections (drops and equipment) | 16,800 subscribers (40% take rate) × $650 | $10.9 million |
| Network core, electronics, contingency | Engineering estimate | $4.6 million |
| **Total** | | **$68.0 million** |

: Capital cost estimate, citywide fiber-to-the-home.

Financed with 25-year revenue bonds at 4.5%, annual debt service would be about $4.6 million.[^debtservice] At an average revenue of $65 per subscriber per month and operating costs of 45% of revenue, each subscriber contributes about $429 per year toward debt service. Covering debt service alone would therefore require roughly **10,700 subscribers, a 25% take rate**, before any reserve for equipment replacement. Comparable municipal systems have reached take rates of 40% to 60% after five years, but the early years carry real risk. That risk is the central reason this brief does not recommend Option A citywide at this time.

## 5. Recommendations

We recommend that the Council pursue a **phased open-access strategy (Option B)**, combined with an immediate **digital equity program (Option D)**:

1. **Commission a detailed feasibility study and business plan** (about $250,000, six months), including a door-to-door take-rate survey in Eastbank and North Ridge. Make any construction decision contingent on its findings.
2. **Build a city-owned fiber backbone** connecting city facilities, schools, libraries, and traffic signals. The backbone is useful on its own (it would replace about $410,000 per year in leased circuits) and is the foundation for any later build.
3. **Apply for a BEAD subgrant in March 2027** for an open-access fiber pilot covering Eastbank and North Ridge (about 7,200 passings, estimated at $11.8 million, of which the city's 25% match would be about $3.0 million).
4. **Launch a digital equity program now**, funded at $1.5 million per year for three years: a $20 monthly service credit for income-qualified households, refurbished laptops through the library, and digital skills classes. Require any provider on a future city network to offer a low-income plan at 100/20 Mbps for no more than $30 per month.
5. **Protect the city's general fund.** Structure any network as a separate enterprise fund with revenue bonds, publish quarterly financial reports, and set explicit take-rate milestones that trigger a review.

## 6. Questions for Council discussion

- What level of financial risk is the Council willing to accept to close the coverage gap faster?
- Should the city prefer an open-access model even if it delays service compared with a single private partner?
- How should the digital equity program be funded after the third year?
