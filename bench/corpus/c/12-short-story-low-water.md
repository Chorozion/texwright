# The Low Water Year

*E. M. Hollis*

> Time is but the stream I go a-fishing in.
>
> — Henry David Thoreau, *Walden*

The town came back in August, after eleven weeks without rain.

It came back the way a word comes back when you have stopped trying to remember it. First the steeple, which had never fully gone under, showed a little more of itself each morning, until the bell louvers were in the air again and swallows went in and out of them. Then the roofline of the feed store. Then, one Tuesday, the top three courses of the bank, its granite gone the green-brown of a pond turtle.

By the second week of August the water had dropped forty-one feet, and the county put up orange fencing at the boat ramp and a sign that said HISTORIC SITE: DO NOT REMOVE ARTIFACTS, and people came from three states to walk on Main Street in Calder, Ohio, which had been at the bottom of Calder Lake since 1984.

Ruth Ansel did not go the first week, or the second. She told her daughter it was a circus, and it was. There were drones. There was a man selling lemonade from a cooler at the top of the ramp. A television crew from Columbus interviewed a woman who had been born in the hospital, which was not in Calder at all but eleven miles away in Bryce, and the woman cried on camera about her lost hometown, and Ruth turned the set off.

"You should see it," her grandson said. Theo was fourteen and had come to stay for the summer because his parents were, in his phrase, "figuring things out." He said it without bitterness, which worried Ruth more than bitterness would have. "Before it fills up again. Grandpa would have gone."

That was true, and he knew it was the one argument that would work, and she respected him for using it.

* * *

They went on a Thursday, early, before the heat and the people. The ramp ended in mud that had dried into plates the size of dinner tables, curled up at the edges like old paper. Below it the lake had pulled back into a channel a quarter mile off, low and the color of weak tea, and between the ramp and the water lay the town.

It was smaller than she remembered. Everyone says that about the places they grew up, but this was smaller in a particular way: it had been sanded. Forty years of silt had softened every edge. The curbs were rounded. The sidewalks had disappeared under a gray skin that cracked when you stepped on it. Trees that had been cut before the flooding stood as black stumps along what had been Second Street, at regular intervals, like the pilings of a pier.

"Which way?" Theo said.

She pointed with her chin, the way her father used to. "Church first. Everything is from the church."

They walked down the middle of Main Street. There were other people, but not many yet: a couple with a metal detector, which was not allowed; a man photographing the bank; two girls in college sweatshirts taking pictures of each other in the doorway of what had been the Rexall. The doorway had no door. None of them had doors. The Army Corps of Engineers had taken off every door and window in the summer of 1983, so that the buildings would fill evenly and not float up and become hazards to navigation. Ruth had watched them do it. She had been twenty-six, and pregnant with Theo's mother, and furious.

The church was Methodist, white clapboard over a stone foundation. The clapboard was gone and the stone remained, and the steeple, which had been stone too, all the way up. She climbed the four front steps and stood where the doors had been. Inside there was nothing. A floor of mud. Light coming in through the window holes in long, dusty bars.

"You got married here," Theo said.

"I did."

"Was it nice?"

She considered. "It was hot. Your grandfather's collar was too tight and he turned purple during the vows. My sister cried so loudly the minister had to stop." She looked at the place where the altar had been. "Yes. It was nice."

* * *

From the church she could find everything, as she'd said. Down the side street toward the river, past the lot where the Grange hall had stood, now only a rectangle of foundation stones. Left at the corner where the Pruitts' dog used to lie in the road and refuse to move for cars. Three houses down on the right.

She would not have known the house without the sycamore. The stump was enormous, wider than she was tall, and the roots spread out over the yard like a hand laid flat on a table. Her father had hung a swing from that tree. She had broken her wrist falling off it when she was seven, and he had carried her the whole way to the doctor on Main Street because the car would not start.

The house itself was gone. The Corps had burned the wood-frame houses, and what was left was the cellar hole, half full of silt, and the front steps, which were concrete and led up to nothing. She stood at the bottom of them for a long time.

Theo didn't say anything. She was grateful. He walked around the cellar hole slowly, looking down into it, then crouched by the steps and brushed at the mud with his fingers.

"Grandma," he said. "There's something here."

It was the boot scraper. A plain iron bar set into two posts, sunk into the concrete beside the bottom step, so you could scrape the mud off your boots before going in. Her mother had insisted on it. Her father had grumbled about it for a week and then installed it on a Sunday afternoon, with Ruth handing him tools, and for the rest of his life he had used it every single time he came in the house, scraping each boot twice, left then right, as if he'd invented it.

It was rusted almost through on one side. Theo rubbed the top of the bar with his thumb and a flake of rust came away and under it the iron was black and smooth.

"Can we take it?" he said.

"No," Ruth said. "The sign."

"Nobody would know."

"I would know." She surprised herself by laughing. "Your great-grandfather would know. He'd come up out of that cellar and tell us to put it back."

So they left it. But she crouched down too, which was hard on her knees and which she would pay for that night, and she put her hand on the bar next to his, and they stayed like that until a heron came down onto the mud flat behind the house and began walking, very slowly and with great seriousness, toward the water.

* * *

They followed the heron down to the river.

Not the lake. The river. That was the thing nobody on television had mentioned: under the lake, the river was still there. When the water dropped far enough the old channel reappeared, the Calder River, narrow and quick between banks of drowned willows, running exactly where it had always run. The lake was only lying on top of it.

There was a flat rock on the near bank that Ruth knew before she saw it. It was where Walt had taught her to fish, the summer before they were married, when she had pretended not to know how so that he would have to stand behind her and hold her wrists. He had known she was pretending. He told her so, years later, on their thirtieth anniversary, and she had been so annoyed that he had known all along that she threw a dinner roll at him across the restaurant.

Walt had fished the lake for thirty-five years after the town went under. He had a little aluminum boat and he went out every Saturday morning from April to October, and he always anchored in the same spot, about a quarter mile off the ramp, and he would not tell anyone why. Ruth knew why. It was right above this rock. He was fishing the river, forty feet down, in the place where he had stood behind her and held her wrists.

She told Theo this. She had not told anyone, not even her daughter. She was not sure why she told him, except that he had been the one to find the boot scraper, and that he had stood at the edge of the cellar hole without saying anything, and that he was fourteen and his parents were figuring things out.

Theo listened. When she finished he looked out at the water for a while.

"Did he catch anything?"

"Hardly ever. Bluegill. He let them all go."

"So he just sat there."

"He just sat there."

Theo nodded as if that made perfect sense. Maybe it did. He picked up a flat stone and skipped it across the river, four times, and it sank in the shallows on the far side where the willows were, and the heron, which had been standing there the whole time, lifted itself into the air without hurry and flew downstream.

* * *

The rain came back on the twenty-ninth of September, and kept coming. By Thanksgiving the water was over the church steps. By Christmas only the top of the steeple showed, and then, in a storm in January, not even that.

Theo went home in late August. His parents had figured things out, or figured out enough; they were selling the house, and he would have two bedrooms now, one in each of their apartments, and he said this on the phone in the same even voice he used for everything. Ruth told him that two bedrooms was a lot of bedrooms. He said that was what he'd been thinking.

In the spring she bought a fishing license for the first time in her life. She didn't buy a boat. Walt's boat was still in the garage, and she had a neighbor's son put new spark plugs in the motor and check the hull for leaks, and on the first Saturday in April she drove to the ramp and put it in the water herself. It took her three tries to start the motor.

She went out a quarter mile and dropped anchor where she guessed the rock would be. She was off by a little, probably. It didn't matter. The river was down there either way, running where it always ran, under everything.

She baited the hook the way Walt had shown her, with her own hands this time, and dropped the line over the side, and sat.
