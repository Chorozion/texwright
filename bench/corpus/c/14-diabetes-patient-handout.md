# Living Well with Type 2 Diabetes

**A guide for patients and families**
Riverside Family Health Clinic · Diabetes Education Program

---

## What is type 2 diabetes?

When you eat, your body breaks food down into sugar (glucose) that goes into your blood. A hormone called **insulin** moves that sugar from your blood into your cells, where it is used for energy.

With type 2 diabetes, your body does not use insulin well, and over time it may not make enough. Sugar builds up in your blood instead of going into your cells. High blood sugar over many years can damage your eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart, and blood vessels.

**The good news:** you can do a lot to control your blood sugar and prevent these problems. Small, steady changes make a real difference.

## Your numbers

Your care team will set targets that are right for you. These are common goals for many adults:

| Test | What it measures | Common goal |
|---|---|---|
| **A1C** | Your average blood sugar over the last 2 to 3 months | Below 7% |
| **Blood sugar before meals** | Sugar level when you haven't eaten for a few hours | 80 to 130 mg/dL |
| **Blood sugar 1 to 2 hours after starting a meal** | How your body handles food | Below 180 mg/dL |
| **Blood pressure** | Pressure in your blood vessels | Below 130/80 |
| **LDL ("bad") cholesterol** | Fat in your blood that can clog arteries | Ask your provider; often below 70 or 100 mg/dL |

## Checking your blood sugar

Your provider will tell you how often to check. Many people check:

- when they first wake up, before eating;
- before meals, or 2 hours after meals; and
- any time they feel shaky, sweaty, or "off."

Write your results in a logbook or app and **bring it to every visit**. Your numbers help your care team adjust your treatment.

> **Tip:** Wash and dry your hands before you check. Sugar on your fingers from food can give a falsely high reading.

## Eating well

You do not need special "diabetic" foods. You need regular meals with the right balance. The **plate method** is an easy way to start. Use a 9-inch plate:

- **Half the plate:** non-starchy vegetables, such as salad, green beans, broccoli, carrots, peppers, or tomatoes
- **One quarter:** lean protein, such as chicken, fish, eggs, beans, tofu, or lean meat
- **One quarter:** carbohydrate foods, such as brown rice, whole-grain bread, potatoes, pasta, corn, or fruit
- **To drink:** water, or another drink with no sugar

### Carbohydrates affect blood sugar the most

Carbohydrates ("carbs") are found in bread, rice, pasta, cereal, potatoes, corn, beans, fruit, milk, yogurt, sweets, and sugary drinks. You don't have to cut them out, but watch how much you eat at one time.

| Choose more often | Choose less often |
|---|---|
| Water, unsweetened tea, black coffee | Regular soda, sweet tea, juice, energy drinks |
| Whole fruit | Fruit juice, canned fruit in syrup |
| Brown rice, oatmeal, whole-grain bread | White bread, white rice, sugary cereal |
| Beans, lentils, nuts | Chips, crackers, pastries |
| Baked, grilled, or steamed foods | Fried foods |

**Sugary drinks raise blood sugar faster than almost anything else.** Switching from regular soda to water is one of the most helpful changes you can make.

## Being active

Physical activity helps your body use insulin better and lowers blood sugar, sometimes for a day or more.

- **Aim for 150 minutes a week** of activity that makes you breathe a little harder, like brisk walking. That's 30 minutes, 5 days a week. Three 10-minute walks count too.
- **Add strength exercises 2 or 3 times a week**, such as resistance bands, light weights, or standing up from a chair 10 times.
- **Break up long sitting.** Get up and move for a few minutes every 30 minutes.
- **A walk after meals** is especially good at lowering after-meal blood sugar.

Talk to your provider before starting a new exercise plan, especially if you have heart problems or numbness in your feet.

## Your medicines

Many people with type 2 diabetes need medicine, and many need more over time. **This is not a failure.** Diabetes changes over the years, and treatment changes with it.

| Medicine type | Examples | How it helps | Things to know |
|---|---|---|---|
| Metformin | Glucophage | Lowers the sugar your liver makes | Take with food. Upset stomach is common at first and usually improves. |
| GLP-1 medicines | Semaglutide (Ozempic), dulaglutide (Trulicity) | Help your body release insulin; reduce appetite | Usually a weekly shot. Nausea is common at first. Can help with weight loss. |
| SGLT2 inhibitors | Empagliflozin (Jardiance), dapagliflozin (Farxiga) | Help your kidneys remove sugar in urine | Drink plenty of water. Keep the genital area clean. Also protect the heart and kidneys. |
| Sulfonylureas | Glipizide, glimepiride | Make your pancreas release more insulin | **Can cause low blood sugar.** Don't skip meals. |
| Insulin | Many types | Replaces or adds to your own insulin | **Can cause low blood sugar.** Your team will teach you how to use it. |

**Never stop a medicine on your own.** If you have side effects or can't afford a medicine, call us. There are often other options.

## Low blood sugar (hypoglycemia)

Low blood sugar is **below 70 mg/dL**. It is most likely if you take insulin or a sulfonylurea.

**Warning signs:** shaking, sweating, fast heartbeat, feeling hungry, dizziness, headache, confusion, crankiness, blurry vision.

### Treat it with the "Rule of 15"

1. Eat or drink **15 grams of fast-acting sugar**, such as:
   - 4 glucose tablets
   - ½ cup (4 oz) of juice or regular (not diet) soda
   - 1 tablespoon of sugar or honey
2. **Wait 15 minutes**, then check your blood sugar again.
3. If it is still below 70, **repeat** steps 1 and 2.
4. Once it is back above 70, eat a snack or meal if your next meal is more than an hour away.

> ⚠️ **Call 911** if someone with diabetes passes out or cannot swallow safely. Do not put food or drink in their mouth.

Always carry glucose tablets or another fast sugar with you, and tell family and coworkers what to do.

## High blood sugar (hyperglycemia)

Signs include being very thirsty, urinating often, feeling tired, and blurry vision. It can be caused by eating more carbs than usual, missing medicine, stress, or illness.

**Call the clinic** if your blood sugar stays above 300 mg/dL on two checks in a row, or above 250 mg/dL for more than a day.

## Sick days

Illness, even a cold, can raise your blood sugar.

- **Keep taking your diabetes medicine** unless your provider tells you otherwise. (If you take an SGLT2 inhibitor or metformin and are vomiting or can't keep fluids down, call us; you may need to pause them.)
- Check your blood sugar **every 4 hours**.
- Drink a cup of water or sugar-free fluid every hour while awake.
- If you can't eat normally, have soup, crackers, applesauce, or juice to get some carbs.
- **Call us** if you are vomiting, have diarrhea for more than 6 hours, or have a fever over 101 °F for more than a day.

## Caring for your feet

Diabetes can reduce feeling in your feet, so you may not notice a cut or blister.

- Look at your feet **every day**, including between your toes and the soles. Use a mirror if needed.
- Wash and dry them well, especially between the toes.
- Wear shoes and socks at all times, even indoors.
- Don't cut corns or calluses yourself.
- **Call us right away** for any sore, cut, blister, redness, or swelling that isn't healing.

## Checkups to keep up with

| Checkup | How often |
|---|---|
| A1C blood test | Every 3 to 6 months |
| Blood pressure check | Every visit |
| Foot check by your provider | Every visit; full exam once a year |
| Dilated eye exam | Once a year (or as your eye doctor advises) |
| Kidney tests (urine and blood) | Once a year |
| Cholesterol | Once a year |
| Dental cleaning | Every 6 months |
| Flu shot | Every fall |
| Other vaccines (pneumonia, hepatitis B, COVID-19) | Ask your provider |

## Questions to ask at your next visit

- What is my latest A1C, and what should my goal be?
- How often should I check my blood sugar?
- Are any of my medicines likely to cause low blood sugar?
- Can I see a dietitian or a diabetes educator?
- Is my insurance covering my supplies and medicines?
